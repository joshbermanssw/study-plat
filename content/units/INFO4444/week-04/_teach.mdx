## What this week is really about

Week 4 turns from *how* a technology evolves (Weeks 2–3) to *where* the ideas come from. For most of the 20th century, the dominant model was *closed* innovation: large firms ran integrated R&D labs (Bell Labs, IBM Yorktown, Xerox PARC, Kodak Research) that owned the entire pipeline from basic science to product launch. By the early 2000s, that model had broken: skilled workers became mobile, venture capital became abundant, university research became commercially viable, and small startups could routinely out-innovate established R&D departments. Henry Chesbrough's *Open Innovation* (Harvard Business School Press, 2003) named the shift and gave us the framework we still use. This week is built around Chesbrough's argument, the **inside-out / outside-in / coupled** taxonomy of innovation flows, the **absorptive capacity** concept that explains why open innovation is harder than it sounds, and a serious critique: is open innovation really new, or just a rebranding of outsourcing? By the end you should be able to (a) state Chesbrough's six principles distinguishing open from closed, (b) place a real firm's innovation behaviour on the inside-out/outside-in/coupled axis, (c) explain Cohen and Levinthal's absorptive-capacity argument, (d) narrate at least two case studies (P&G Connect+Develop, IBM patent licensing, Apple as a *counterexample*), and (e) state the rebranded-outsourcing critique and engage with it.

## The closed innovation paradigm and why it broke

The closed innovation model treated R&D as an *internal* function. The implicit contract was: hire the smartest people, give them the best lab equipment, fund their work for decades, file every patent defensively, integrate forwards into production and sales, and capture all the value yourself. Bell Labs under AT&T's regulated monopoly is the canonical case: from 1925 to 1984 it produced the transistor (1947), information theory (Shannon 1948), the laser (1960), Unix (1969), the CCD (1969), the C programming language (1972), and seven Nobel Prizes — all funded by the AT&T monopoly rents and almost all kept inside the firm. IBM, Xerox PARC, DuPont Central Research, Merck, and Kodak ran similar models. Chesbrough's six principles of the *closed* paradigm:

1. The smart people in our field work for us.
2. To profit from R&D, we must discover, develop, and ship it ourselves.
3. If we discover it ourselves, we'll get it to market first.
4. The company that gets to market first will win.
5. If we create the most and best ideas in the industry, we'll win.
6. We should control our IP so that competitors don't profit from our ideas.

By the late 1990s, every one of these had become wrong. **Why?**

- **Mobility of skilled workers.** Knowledge that used to be "locked in" to a firm via lifetime employment now walked out the door when an engineer changed jobs every 4–5 years. Silicon Valley's no-non-compete legal regime (California) made this especially severe.
- **Abundant venture capital.** The Bayh-Dole Act (1980, US) let universities patent federally funded research; the 1980s VC boom funded thousands of startups. A frustrated researcher could now leave a corporate lab, raise $5M, and *compete* with the lab they left.
- **More capable suppliers and partners.** Specialist firms (Foxconn for manufacturing, AWS for infrastructure, Stripe for payments) made integration unnecessary.
- **Lower information costs.** The internet, scientific publishing, and conference circuits made tracking external research much cheaper.

The closed model started to leak ideas without capturing their value. The Xerox PARC story is the canonical horror case: Xerox invented the GUI, the mouse, Ethernet, the laser printer, and the workstation — and shipped almost none of them. Steve Jobs famously saw the Alto on a 1979 visit and built the Macintosh from what he saw; Adobe was founded by ex-PARC researchers; 3Com was founded by Bob Metcalfe (PARC); Sun by Bill Joy and others. Chesbrough's argument: the *internal* metric ("did this project ship a Xerox product?") was the wrong metric. The *correct* metric was ("did this idea create economic value somewhere, and did we capture a share of it?").

## Chesbrough's open innovation paradigm

Chesbrough's six principles of *open* innovation, deliberately set against the closed list:

1. Not all the smart people work for us; we need to work with smart people inside *and* outside our company.
2. External R&D can create significant value; internal R&D is needed to claim some portion of that value.
3. We do not have to originate the research to profit from it.
4. Building a better business model is better than getting to market first.
5. If we make the best use of internal *and* external ideas, we will win.
6. We should profit from others' use of our IP, and we should license in others' IP whenever it advances our business model.

The most important shift here is **principle 4**: from "first to market" (the closed assumption) to "best business model". A firm with a better business model can profit from an idea it didn't invent. Apple did not invent the MP3 player, the smartphone, the tablet, or the smartwatch — it produced the best business model for each of them. This recasts innovation strategy: the goal is not to *generate* the most ideas, it is to *combine* internal and external ideas into a profitable system.

## The three flows: inside-out, outside-in, coupled

Chesbrough later (with Eichenholz, Vanhaverbeke et al., 2006 onwards) gave us a clean three-flow taxonomy.

**Outside-in (inbound) open innovation.** External ideas, technologies, and IP flow *into* the firm. This includes licensing in patents, acquiring startups, sponsoring university research, running crowdsourcing competitions, joining consortia, and using suppliers as innovation partners. **P&G's Connect+Develop** programme (launched 2001 under CEO A.G. Lafley) is the textbook case: P&G set a target that 50% of new product ideas would come from external sources, built an internal liaison network of about 70 "technology entrepreneurs", and ran an external website (connect.pg.com) inviting submissions. Pringles Prints (the printed-image potato chip) came from a bakery in Bologna, Italy that had developed the edible-ink printing technology — a 3-month integration vs an estimated 2-year internal development.

**Inside-out (outbound) open innovation.** Internal ideas, technologies, and IP flow *out* of the firm — through licensing, spin-offs, joint ventures, and selling unused IP. **IBM's patent licensing programme** is the canonical case: after refocusing in the 1990s under Lou Gerstner, IBM systematically monetised its enormous patent portfolio (still the world's largest), earning more than $1 billion per year in licensing revenue. IBM also pioneered the open-source pattern: in 2000 it committed $1 billion to Linux, releasing IP and engineering effort *outward* while building services revenue around the open platform. Eli Lilly's spin-out InnoCentive (2001) is another inside-out artefact: Lilly externalised problem-solving by creating a marketplace where solvers anywhere could attempt their problems.

**Coupled open innovation.** Bidirectional flows in genuine partnerships: joint ventures, strategic alliances, university-industry research centres, supplier co-development. Boeing's Dreamliner programme (2003 onwards) was a coupled-innovation experiment at extreme scale, with risk-sharing partners across multiple countries co-developing major aircraft sections. (It also illustrates the *risks* of coupled innovation: coordination costs blew out, the programme ran years late, and the partners' problems compounded.) The Toyota-supplier *keiretsu* system is another canonical case: Toyota and its suppliers co-develop component technologies and share productivity gains.

## Apple as the counter-example

It is easy to read Chesbrough and conclude that open is *always* better. Apple is the central counter-example. From the iPhone forward, Apple has been *more* closed, not less: tight vertical integration (CPU silicon since A4 in 2010, M1 in 2020), proprietary OS, controlled distribution (App Store), tight supplier discipline, deliberate isolation from cross-licensing pacts. And Apple has been the most profitable consumer-electronics firm in history.

Why does Apple work as a closed firm in an era of open innovation? Three answers.

1. **Apple is not closed at the *idea* level — it is closed at the *system integration* level.** Apple licenses ARM (chip architecture), uses LLVM (open compiler), runs WebKit (open browser engine, originally from KDE), and adopts external standards (USB-C, AirPlay-as-Matter). The proprietary moat is the *integration* and *user experience* — not the underlying inputs.
2. **In categories with strong design-driven user experience and high willingness to pay**, tight vertical control can outperform modular openness. Apple's segment is the high-margin top of the market, where customers will pay for integration polish.
3. **Apple operates as a *system integrator* of open inputs.** It can be open on most components and still close at the system level — a strategic position only a few firms can afford because it requires both deep platform engineering and a brand that supports premium pricing.

For exam purposes: do not present Apple as "ignoring" the open paradigm. Present it as a *system integrator* using open inputs at the component level and a closed system at the user-experience level. That is more accurate and more sophisticated.

## Absorptive capacity: why open isn't free

The dirty secret of open innovation is that it is harder than it looks. The most cited result here is Wesley Cohen and Daniel Levinthal, *"Absorptive Capacity: A New Perspective on Learning and Innovation"* (Administrative Science Quarterly, 1990). Their argument:

> A firm's ability to identify, assimilate, and exploit knowledge from the environment depends on its *prior related knowledge*. Absorptive capacity is largely a by-product of the firm's own R&D investment.

In other words: to absorb external ideas you must have your own internal R&D capability that *understands* those ideas. A firm with no internal pharma research cannot effectively license in a pharmaceutical compound — it cannot evaluate the compound, design the trials, or integrate the result. P&G's Connect+Develop *worked* because P&G had a strong internal R&D base (about 8,000 internal researchers when the programme launched) that could absorb and integrate external contributions. A firm that tries to *replace* internal R&D with open innovation typically fails: it loses the ability to evaluate what it is receiving.

The implication is sharper than it first appears: open innovation is a *complement* to internal R&D, not a *substitute* for it. The firms that benefit most from open innovation are those with strong existing R&D capabilities. This is awkward, because the firms that *want* open innovation the most are usually those whose internal R&D is weakest — and they will benefit from it the least.

A useful corollary: organisational design for open innovation requires explicit *boundary-spanning roles* — people whose job is to translate between internal and external worlds. P&G's "technology entrepreneurs" were a deliberate investment in absorptive capacity at the boundary.

## IP, boundaries, and the patent-as-currency view

In the closed paradigm, IP is *defensive* — patents block competitors from using your inventions. In the open paradigm, IP is *currency* — patents are assets you can license out, license in, swap in cross-licensing pacts, and use to ground revenue-sharing partnerships. This re-conception changes patent strategy completely (we will go deep on this in Week 6).

A canonical example of IP-as-currency: the modern smartphone industry runs on cross-licensing. Apple, Samsung, Qualcomm, Nokia, Ericsson, Huawei all hold large patent portfolios in cellular standards; almost everyone licenses to everyone, and the *net* cash flow is much smaller than the gross flow. Without cross-licensing, every smartphone would face hundreds of patent claims; with cross-licensing, each firm's patents are mostly defensive *insurance* against being sued.

A second canonical example: **defensive patent pools** like LOT Network, Open Invention Network (Linux patents), and the Eclipse Public Licence patent grant. These are open-innovation structures that *commit* members to share IP within a defined scope, reducing the dead-weight cost of cross-firm litigation.

## Lead-user innovation: the user as innovator

A complementary tradition we will pick up properly in Week 5 is **Eric von Hippel**'s lead-user innovation framework (*Sources of Innovation*, 1988; *Democratizing Innovation*, 2005). Von Hippel showed that in many industries (scientific instruments, sports equipment, software), users — not manufacturers — are the original sources of innovation. *Lead users* are users who face needs that the market will face later, and who benefit substantially from solving those needs themselves. They tend to *develop their own solutions*, which manufacturers can then commercialise.

This is a form of open innovation that runs *upstream* into the user community rather than sideways into peer firms or downstream into universities. It is a major intellectual root of crowdsourcing, the maker movement, open-source software, and the modern "community-driven product" pattern.

## Two further case studies you should know

**Procter & Gamble Connect+Develop (2001–present).** Launched under A.G. Lafley as part of "Organisation 2005". Goal: 50% of P&G's innovation should involve external collaboration. The programme has multiple modules:

- *Top 10 needs lists* posted publicly, inviting external solutions.
- *Technology entrepreneurs* embedded in business units, scouting universities, startups, and suppliers.
- *Yet2.com, NineSigma, InnoCentive* — third-party marketplaces P&G uses to source problems.
- *Reverse-direction*: P&G licenses out underutilised internal IP.

By 2010 P&G reported R&D productivity (innovations per R&D dollar) had nearly doubled. The model has been copied (with variable success) by Unilever, GSK, Mondelez, and many others. The critique: Connect+Develop worked partly because P&G's internal R&D capability was already enormous (point about absorptive capacity above), and the productivity gains were partly an accounting effect (more inputs counted as innovation).

**IBM and Linux (2000).** IBM committed $1 billion in 2000 to Linux development. The decision looked counter-intuitive — a closed-paradigm IBM would have invested in its own proprietary Unix (AIX) and tried to lock customers in. Open-paradigm IBM saw that Linux was going to win the server OS market regardless, and that IBM's profit pool was no longer in OS licences but in *services* around the OS. By investing in Linux and contributing engineering, IBM (a) prevented any single competitor (notably Microsoft) from dominating server software, (b) reduced its own OS development cost, (c) positioned itself as the credible enterprise services partner for the now-open platform. Estimated return: $5+ billion of services and hardware revenue per year over the 2000s. This is open innovation *as competitive strategy*, not as charity.

## Critique: is open innovation just rebranded outsourcing?

The single most important critique of Chesbrough's framework — and one any honours student should be able to articulate — is that *open innovation* is partly a rebranding of practices firms have done for centuries (technology licensing, supplier co-development, scientific collaboration, technology scouting). The critics' charge:

- *Open innovation* is a *practitioner term* with weak academic specification — it is hard to operationalise and harder to test.
- Empirical studies (Laursen-Salter 2006, Chesbrough-Crowther 2006) show real productivity gains, but the gains are concentrated among large, R&D-intensive firms — exactly the firms that practiced this stuff under other names already.
- The framework can be cover for *cost-cutting outsourcing*: a firm reduces its internal R&D, claims to be "open", and replaces deep capability with thin contractor relationships. The result is often a hollowed-out firm with little absorptive capacity left.
- The framework is silent on *power*: who captures the value created by open innovation? In many cases it is the platform-owner (Apple's App Store), not the contributing innovators (independent developers).

The empirical literature post-2010 has refined the picture: open innovation works in some industries (pharmaceuticals, consumer goods, software) better than others (specialty chemicals, heavy machinery); works at some firm sizes (large with strong R&D) better than others (small without absorptive capacity); and works for some kinds of innovation (modular, where interfaces are clear) better than others (architectural, where coordination costs spike).

For your essay: take Chesbrough seriously *and* the critique seriously. The honest position is that open innovation is a *real* shift in degree (more permeability, more deliberate boundary-spanning, more IP-as-currency), supported by *real* structural changes (mobility, VC, university capability), but is neither universal nor simple — and is sometimes used as cover for hollowing-out.

## Inside-out / outside-in / coupled: a checklist for case studies

When you analyse a firm's open-innovation behaviour, work through this checklist. Most exam answers benefit from explicit use of all three flows.

```

                        ───────────         ──────────          ───────
Direction                Internal IP out    External IP in      Bidirectional
                                                                
Mechanisms               Licensing out,     Licensing in,       Joint ventures,
                         spin-offs, IP      acquiring startups,  strategic alliances,
                         sales              crowdsourcing,       co-development
                                            sponsored research

Example                  IBM patent         P&G Connect+        Toyota-supplier
                         licensing,         Develop, Eli Lilly  keiretsu, Boeing
                         Eli Lilly's        InnoCentive          787 partners
                         InnoCentive

Value-capture risk       Low (you choose    Medium (you must    High (coordination,
                         what to release)   integrate)           contract design)

Required capability      IP management,     Absorptive          Alliance management,
                         deal-making        capacity,            governance design
                                            integration
```

The most sophisticated open-innovation strategies use all three deliberately. Microsoft post-2014 (under Satya Nadella) is a contemporary example: inside-out (open-sourcing .NET, contributing to Linux/Kubernetes), outside-in (acquiring GitHub, OpenAI investment), coupled (Azure-OpenAI deep partnership). Apple, by contrast, uses heavy outside-in (component sourcing, ARM licensing) but very little inside-out (it does not license iOS), giving it a distinctive and unusual position.

## Applying open innovation: Microsoft 2014–2024 as a worked case

The clearest contemporary case of an open-innovation pivot at scale is Microsoft under Satya Nadella (CEO from February 2014). Pre-2014 Microsoft was an archetypal closed-innovation firm: proprietary OS, proprietary Office, hostile to Linux (Steve Ballmer famously called Linux "a cancer"), defensive patent litigation. Under Nadella, the firm pivoted to all three open-innovation flows simultaneously.

*Inside-out.* Microsoft open-sourced .NET Core (2014), Visual Studio Code (2015), TypeScript (2012, pre-Nadella but expanded under him), the Edge browser's rendering engine (Chromium-based from 2020), PowerShell (2016). The Linux Foundation joined as a Platinum member in 2016 — a symbolic and material reversal.

*Outside-in.* GitHub acquired (2018, $7.5B), bringing not just the platform but the entire developer-tools ecosystem into Microsoft. LinkedIn acquired (2016). Mojang/Minecraft acquired (2014). And — the headline outside-in move of the decade — the OpenAI investment (initial $1B in 2019, expanded to multi-billion-dollar partnership through 2024) integrated frontier LLM capability with Microsoft's product surface.

*Coupled.* The Azure-OpenAI partnership is a deeply coupled relationship: OpenAI gets compute and capital, Microsoft gets exclusive access to deployed models and the ability to embed them in Microsoft 365 Copilot, GitHub Copilot, and Azure AI services. Co-development, joint product, shared go-to-market.

The financial outcome: Microsoft's market capitalisation went from ~$300B in 2014 to over $3T in 2024. Some of this is broader tech revaluation, but the *qualitative* shift to open is widely credited as a major contributor. The case is interesting because it shows open innovation *implemented by a previously closed incumbent* — empirically possible, but requiring cultural reset that took years.

The case also illustrates *Cohen-Levinthal absorptive capacity*: Microsoft's enormous internal engineering capability (research labs, OS expertise, cloud infrastructure) is what *enabled* the firm to absorb open-source contributions, GitHub's ecosystem, and OpenAI's models. A smaller, less capable firm could not have integrated this much external innovation in a decade.
