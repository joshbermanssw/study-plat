## What this week is really about

Week 3 introduces what is, by some distance, the most famous (and most abused) framework in innovation management: Clayton Christensen's theory of **disruptive innovation**, set out in *The Innovator's Dilemma* (Harvard Business School Press, 1997) and extended in *The Innovator's Solution* (2003). The framework's claim is striking: well-managed, customer-listening, profit-maximising firms reliably fail in the face of certain kinds of new technology — *not despite* being well-managed, *because* of it. The same rationality that makes them dominant in good times is the trap that destroys them when a disruptive entrant arrives. This week pairs the disruption framework with **Everett Rogers's Diffusion of Innovations** (Rogers 1962, 5th edition 2003), which gives us the adopter-category vocabulary you have probably already met (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards), and **Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm** (Moore 1991), which adapts Rogers specifically for high-tech products. By the end of the overview, you should be able to (a) state Christensen's mechanism in three sentences, (b) draw the disruption diagram with all four trajectories, (c) place a real case study (steel mini-mills, hard drives, retail bookselling, low-cost airlines, Netflix) onto the diagram, (d) recite Rogers's five adopter categories with their percentages, and (e) state at least one serious critique of disruption theory — because Jill Lepore (2014) and the empirical literature have given the framework a hard time, and an honours-level answer engages with that.

## Sustaining vs disruptive: the core distinction

Christensen's starting move is to partition innovations into two categories *by their effect on the existing market*, not by their technical magnitude.

A **sustaining innovation** improves the performance of an established product *along the dimensions that mainstream customers already value*. It can be incremental (better camera in next year's iPhone) or radical (a new hard-disk-drive head technology that gives 10× areal density). The defining feature is that it makes the *existing customer* happier on the *existing performance dimension*. Sustaining innovations almost always favour incumbents: they have the customer relationships, the brand, the distribution, the complementary assets, and the deep pockets to fund R&D. Christensen's data — five generations of hard-disk-drive technology, 1976–1994 — showed that incumbents win sustaining-innovation contests roughly 100% of the time, even when the technology change is dramatic.

A **disruptive innovation** does something different. It introduces a product that *underperforms* the incumbent's offering on the dimensions mainstream customers care about — but is *better* on some new dimension (cheaper, smaller, simpler, more convenient). Initially, mainstream customers ignore it because it is worse on the metrics they value. The disruptor is therefore relegated to *fringe* markets: customers who are over-served by the incumbent's product (low-end disruption) or who could not afford or use the incumbent's product at all (new-market disruption). Inside those fringes, the disruptor improves on its own trajectory. Over time, it improves *enough* on the mainstream dimensions to satisfy mainstream customers — while remaining cheaper or simpler or more convenient. At that point, mainstream customers defect, and the incumbent — whose cost structure is calibrated to high-margin mainstream products — cannot follow.

The two types matter because they have *opposite* implications for incumbent action. Sustaining innovations should be aggressively funded. Disruptive innovations look unappealing precisely because they offer worse products to smaller markets at lower margins — exactly the wrong things to invest in if you optimise for next-quarter shareholder return. The framework's punchline: rational, well-managed firms *correctly* ignore disruptive technologies *until it is too late*.

## The disruption diagram

The single most important diagram in INFO4444 — burn it into memory.

```
   Performance
       │
       │                                          ╱  Incumbent's
       │                                        ╱    sustaining trajectory
       │                                      ╱       (often exceeds what
       │   ────────────────────────────────╱──        most customers need)
       │                                  ╱
       │       most demanding         ╱
       │       customers' needs   ╱
       │                       ╱            ╱  Disruptor's trajectory
       │  ─────────────────╱───────────────╱─    (starts below mainstream
       │                ╱                ╱        needs, improves rapidly)
       │       least demanding         ╱
       │       customers' needs    ╱
       │                       ╱
       │                    ╱
       │                  ╱
       │              ╱
       │  ─────────╱─────────────────────────  Time / cumulative improvement
       └────────────────────────────────────►
```

Four lines:

1. **Incumbent's sustaining trajectory** — steep, often steeper than the customer can absorb. Incumbents over-shoot demand because they keep adding features to justify higher prices in higher-margin segments.
2. **Disruptor's trajectory** — starts below the *least demanding* customer's needs, climbs steeply because the technology is young (early in its S-curve).
3. **Most demanding customer's needs** — top of the band; rises slowly because human needs do not grow as fast as technology can be improved.
4. **Least demanding customer's needs** — bottom of the band; also rises slowly.

The disruption *happens* when the disruptor's trajectory enters the customer-need band from below — first satisfying the least demanding customers (who defect first), then the median customer, then eventually the most demanding. The incumbent's product is still "better" on the original performance dimension, but the disruptor is now "good enough" *and* cheaper/simpler/more convenient. The market tips.

## Low-end disruption vs new-market disruption

Christensen refined the framework in *The Innovator's Solution* (2003) to distinguish two flavours.

**Low-end disruption** attacks the bottom of an existing market. The disruptor offers a product that satisfies the over-served low-end customer at a lower price point, then climbs up-market. Steel mini-mills are the canonical case: in 1965, mini-mills made rebar (low-grade reinforcing steel) — the integrated steel mills happily ceded that low-margin segment. Mini-mills then climbed to angle iron, then to structural steel, then to sheet steel. By 2000, Nucor had eaten 50% of the US steel industry from below. Every step up-market, integrated mills "fled to higher ground" — and each retreat *raised* their average margin, making the strategy look rational quarter-by-quarter, until there was no higher ground left.

**New-market disruption** does not attack an existing market — it creates a new one *adjacent* to it, by serving non-consumers. Transistor radios (1955) did not initially compete with vacuum-tube radios; they competed with *no radio at all* for teenagers who could not afford or were not allowed a household radio. The PC (1981) did not compete with the mainframe initially; it competed with *no computer at all* for people who could never have justified mainframe access. New-market disruption is harder for incumbents to see precisely because it does not show up as competition for existing customers — until it has built a market base large enough to climb into the incumbent's territory.

## Why incumbents fail: the resource-allocation argument

Christensen's *mechanism* — and this is the part that makes the theory more than a war story — is grounded in **resource-allocation theory** (Bower 1970, *Managing the Resource Allocation Process*). Inside a large firm, capital and talent flow toward projects that promise the highest risk-adjusted return *given the firm's existing customers and cost structure*. A disruptive project loses every internal contest:

- *Margin filter.* Disruptive projects offer lower margins than the firm's average. They flunk the gross-margin hurdle that finance departments enforce.
- *Customer filter.* Existing customers — the ones the sales force talks to — say they do not want the disruptive product. Of course they don't: it is worse on the dimensions they care about.
- *Market-size filter.* The disruptive market is initially tiny. A $50M new market is irrelevant to a $5B firm; its CFO will not let resources flow there.
- *Talent filter.* The firm's best engineers prefer to work on the sustaining roadmap, which is bigger, better-funded, and more prestigious.

The cruel irony: each filter is *individually rational*. The firm's resource-allocation system is not broken — it is functioning exactly as designed, optimising for the firm's current value-creation system. The problem is that the *system* is wrong for a disruptive context. This is why Christensen's prescription is not "be smarter" or "listen harder" — it is *structural separation*: spin out an autonomous unit with its own P&L, its own cost structure, and freedom to serve the disruptive market with disruptive economics. IBM PC division (separated from mainframes in 1981) is the textbook positive example; almost every department store's attempt to "do e-commerce inside the firm" is a negative one.

## Four case studies you must be able to narrate

**1. Disk drives (1976–1994).** Christensen's PhD thesis empirical core. Every generation — 14″ → 8″ → 5.25″ → 3.5″ → 2.5″ — the leading firms of the previous generation failed to lead the next. The new generation was always *worse* on the dimensions current customers (mainframe makers, then minicomputer makers, then PC makers) cared about (capacity, cost per MB), but *better* on a dimension new customers cared about (size, weight, power consumption). Each transition was driven by a *new application* (mainframes → minicomputers → desktops → laptops), and each transition killed the incumbent.

**2. Steel mini-mills (1965–2000).** Nucor and other mini-mill operators used electric-arc furnaces to melt scrap steel, producing rebar at lower cost than integrated mills. Integrated mills (US Steel, Bethlehem) happily ceded rebar — gross margins were ~7%, the lowest in their product mix. After ceding, average margins went *up*, which made the move look brilliant in quarterly reports. Mini-mills then advanced to angle iron, then structural steel, then sheet steel. Each time the integrated mills retreated, their average margin temporarily rose, then collapsed when the next category was overtaken. By 2000 most US integrated mills were bankrupt or merged.

**3. Retail bookselling (1995–2010).** Borders and Barnes & Noble — themselves disruptors of independent bookstores in the 1980s — were disrupted by Amazon. Amazon initially offered worse selection in the *physical experience* dimension (no browsing, no coffee shop, slow delivery), but better on *breadth* (millions of titles vs ~150,000 in a superstore) and *price*. Amazon then introduced new-market dimensions (recommendations, used-book marketplace, Kindle e-books). Borders filed for bankruptcy in 2011.

**4. Low-cost airlines (1971–present).** Southwest Airlines (founded 1967) operated point-to-point routes between secondary airports with single aircraft type, fast turnarounds, no meals, no first class. On the *legacy* dimensions (route network, schedule frequency, business-class comfort), they were worse than American/United/Delta. On the *new* dimensions (price, reliability, simplicity), they were better. They ate the short-haul leisure market entirely, then climbed into business markets. Ryanair did the same in Europe; AirAsia in Southeast Asia. Note that Qantas's competitive response — Jetstar (2003) — is a *deliberate* separated-disruption strategy à la Christensen.

A fifth, contemporary case worth knowing: **Netflix vs Blockbuster (2000–2010)**. Netflix's mail-order DVD service offered worse selection on Blockbuster's mainstream dimension (immediate gratification) but better on new dimensions (no late fees, long-tail catalogue, monthly subscription). Blockbuster could see the threat — they were offered Netflix for $50M in 2000 and declined — but their store-based cost structure could not adapt. They filed for bankruptcy in 2010.

## Rogers's Diffusion of Innovations

While Christensen explains *why* incumbents lose, Rogers explains *how the adopter population is structured* — and therefore *who you sell to first*. Everett Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962, 5th edition 2003), drew on his earlier work as a rural sociologist studying farmers' adoption of hybrid seed corn in 1950s Iowa. He generalised what he saw into a five-category adopter population, with the famous percentages.

```
   Adopter
   density
      │
      │              ╱─╮
      │            ╱     ╮
      │          ╱        ╲                  ← time
      │        ╱            ╲
      │      ╱                ╲
      │    ╱                    ╲
      │  ╱                        ╲
      │╱   ↑    ↑      ↑      ↑    ↑
      │   2.5% 13.5%   34%    34%   16%
      │  Innov. Early  Early  Late  Laggards
      │        Adopt.  Maj.   Maj.
      └──────────────────────────────────► Time of adoption
```

- **Innovators (2.5%)** — venturesome, risk-tolerant, technically savvy, often well-resourced and well-networked outside the local community. They will try a product because it is *new*. Pre-launch beta users, Hacker News commenters.
- **Early adopters (13.5%)** — opinion leaders inside the local community, respected, judgement-driven (not novelty-driven). They are the most important segment to win: the *majority* watches them. CIO of a respected company, influential lead user, "the cool kid".
- **Early majority (34%)** — deliberate, pragmatic, adopt before the average citizen but only after the technology is clearly proven. The pivotal segment in volume terms. "Show me the case studies."
- **Late majority (34%)** — sceptical, adopt only under social or economic pressure. They adopt because *not* adopting is now embarrassing or costly.
- **Laggards (16%)** — traditional, suspicious of change, isolated from interpersonal networks. They may never adopt; or adopt only when the technology has become invisible (electricity, refrigeration).

Rogers also gave us the **five attributes that predict rate of adoption** — examinable on their own:

1. **Relative advantage** — is it better than what it replaces?
2. **Compatibility** — does it fit existing values, experience, workflows?
3. **Simplicity** (vs complexity) — easy to understand and use?
4. **Trialability** — can adopters try it on a small scale before committing?
5. **Observability** — are the results visible to others, so social proof can spread?

Plus **change-agent effort** — explicit marketing/persuasion by the innovating organisation. QWERTY vs Dvorak (Week 2) is a Rogers case: Dvorak had relative advantage but failed on compatibility, trialability, and observability — and there was no committed change agent.

## Moore's Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991, revised 2014) is *Rogers applied to discontinuous innovations in business-to-business technology*. Moore noticed something Rogers's smooth bell curve hid: in real high-tech markets there is a *gap* — a chasm — between the early adopters and the early majority. Early adopters buy on *vision* (this technology will give us a leap forward); early majority buys on *references* (other firms like us have done this successfully). The two groups speak different languages. A startup that has won a string of early-adopter customers cannot use those wins to persuade the early majority, because the early majority does not consider visionaries to be "people like us".

```
   Adoption
   curve
   density
      │           ╭──╮
      │         ╭     ╮
      │       ╭         ╲
      │     ╭              ╲
      │   ╱                  ╲
      │ ╱   │  ¦│ CHASM │¦    ╲
      │     │  ¦ ←──── →¦      ╲
      │ Innov ¦ Early   ¦Early  ╲ Late   Laggards
      │  +    ¦Adopt.   ¦Maj.    Maj.
      └──────────────────────────────► Time
```

Moore's prescription is the **bowling-alley / tornado strategy**: pick a single *beachhead* vertical (one industry, one geography, one use case), serve it end-to-end with a whole-product offering, win 100% of that niche, then use that beachhead as the reference base to attack adjacent verticals. Salesforce's beachhead was the small/mid-market sales-force-automation segment; HubSpot's was inbound marketing for SMBs; Slack's was developer teams. Picking the beachhead is the hardest strategic decision a startup makes in its early scale-up phase.

## The Lepore critique and the empirical record

In 2014, Harvard historian Jill Lepore published a long, sharp critique in *The New Yorker* ("The Disruption Machine") arguing that Christensen's case studies are cherry-picked, the data is selective, and the term *disruption* has become a meaningless marketing buzzword. King-Tucci (2015) and Sood-Tellis (2011) ran systematic empirical tests and found that the disruption pattern, while real, occurs in a minority of industry transitions — many incumbents do successfully manage discontinuities, and many "disruptive" entrants fail rather than triumph. Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh's 2015 MIT Sloan Management Review survey of 77 cases that Christensen had labelled "disruptive" found that only **9%** fit the strict definition.

What survives the critique? Three things.

1. **The mechanism is genuine.** Resource allocation, margin filters, and customer-listening *can* prevent incumbents from adopting genuinely new technologies. The disruption *story* is a real type, even if it is not as universal as Christensen claimed.
2. **The framework is most useful as a diagnostic, not a prediction.** Use it to ask whether a new technology has the *structural* features (different cost basis, different market segment, climbing trajectory) that *could* produce disruption — not to assume it *will*.
3. **The framework is over-applied.** Uber is not really a Christensen disruptor (it competes head-on with taxis at higher quality and similar price); the iPhone is not (it competed at the high end first); Tesla is debatable (started high-end and worked down). Be precise about the technical definition before using the word.

For your essays: *use* Christensen, but with intellectual honesty about the limits.

## Applying disruption: a current-case worked example

Apply the framework to a contemporary IT case: *streaming media services 2007–2024*.

In 2007, Netflix (mail-order DVD subscription) launched a streaming service as a free add-on. The streaming offering was *worse* than DVD on the mainstream dimensions of the time (DVD catalogue depth, video quality, no rebuffering on slow broadband). On *new* dimensions — instant gratification, no shipping wait, multi-device portability — it was better.

The disruption diagram applied:

- *Incumbent product*: cable TV + DVD rental. Performance dimension: catalogue depth, broadcast quality.
- *Disruptor product*: streaming. Initially worse on those dimensions but better on convenience.
- *Customer-need bands*: most demanding viewers (cinephile, sports fans) initially stayed with cable + DVD. Least demanding (casual viewers, students with thin budgets) moved to streaming first.
- *Trajectory*: streaming improved rapidly on the originally weak dimensions (catalogue depth via licensing then original production; quality via codec improvements and broadband expansion). By 2015 it satisfied median viewer needs; by 2020 it had over-shot most needs.

Each subsequent entrant played a Christensen-shaped role: Hulu (2008), Amazon Prime Video (2011), Disney+ (2019), HBO Max (2020). Cable TV cord-cutting accelerated. Some incumbents adapted (Disney repositioned around D+, betting on owned IP); some declined (legacy cable bundles).

Note however that the streaming-vs-cable story has elements that don't fit Christensen perfectly — Netflix arrived from outside the cable industry (not a low-end entrant inside it), and the cost structure shift (variable streaming vs fixed-channel bundling) is partly a business-model change rather than a pure technology disruption. This is a useful case in exam answers because it lets you *apply* Christensen and *engage with the limits of the framework* in the same paragraph.
