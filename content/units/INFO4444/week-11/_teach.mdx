## What this week is really about

Week 11 is the unit's *practitioner* week — a synthesis of the industry-perspective guest-speaker material with the academic literature on founder behaviour, storytelling, sales, persistence, and the practical artefacts (pitch decks, lean canvases, business model canvases) that founders use day-to-day. The intellectual move is the inverse of Weeks 1–10: instead of building up frameworks from theory and testing them on cases, we now look at what founders *actually do* and ask which frameworks survive contact with the practice. By the end of the overview you should be able to (a) recite the most-replicated findings on founder traits, (b) explain the survivorship-bias problem in entrepreneurship research, (c) walk through the standard 10-slide pitch deck and explain what each slide is testing, (d) compare the Lean Canvas and Business Model Canvas and explain when to use each, (e) state the three skills the industry consistently flags as under-taught (storytelling, sales, founder psychology), and (f) place a real Australian or international founder narrative in the unit's frameworks.

## The industry perspective and what it tends to emphasise

Guest-speaker lectures in entrepreneurship units tend to converge on a small number of themes regardless of the specific industry or founder. Five themes that recur:

**1. Storytelling.** Founders sell their company many times a day — to investors, employees, customers, partners, journalists, and themselves. The story is not optional; it is the *core artefact* of a startup's early stage. A clear, memorable narrative ("we are bringing X to Y so that Z") aligns the team, attracts capital, recruits talent, and reduces customer-acquisition friction. Nancy Duarte (*Resonate*, 2010) and Andy Raskin's work on strategic narrative are the practitioner references. The academic frame: storytelling functions as *coordination technology* — it lowers the cost of getting many people aligned around a single, often abstract, future.

**2. Sales.** A consistent industry observation: technical founders systematically under-invest in sales. Engineering builds the product; sales sells it; founders often deprioritise sales as "not their job". The empirical record is unsentimental: companies that succeed have founders who do sales themselves, at least until the product-market-fit signal is unambiguous. Steve Blank's "get out of the building" instruction is essentially a sales-development exercise.

**3. Founder psychology and persistence.** The startup journey is *long* and *emotionally taxing*. Founder mental-health literature (Michael Freeman's 2015 study found 49% of entrepreneurs report a mental-health condition vs ~7% of the general US population) and Ben Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* (2014) document the structural psychological challenge: founders make consequential decisions with incomplete information under social and financial pressure for years. The frameworks of Weeks 1-10 are silent on this; in practice, founder *resilience* (or its absence) is often what determines whether the company survives long enough to reach product-market fit.

**4. Network and luck.** Most successful founders had access to a network — investors, mentors, customers, talented co-founders — that they did *not* primarily build through merit but rather through prior employment, education, geography, or family. The networks matter enormously in early stages (who funded the company, who joined the team, who provided the first customer reference). Acknowledging the role of network and luck is intellectually important; it complicates the "anyone can be a founder" rhetoric that dominates startup culture.

**5. Execution speed.** Industry repeatedly emphasises *velocity* — the speed at which experiments can be designed, run, and learned from. This corresponds to the lean-startup emphasis on cycle time but is usually voiced in more visceral terms ("ship daily", "decisions in hours not weeks", "the company that learns fastest wins"). It is empirically correlated with success at the venture stage.

## Founder traits research

What does the academic literature say about founder traits? Three findings to know.

**1. The "founder genius" myth is not supported.** Studies (Pierre Azoulay et al. 2020, *"Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship"*) found that the *average age* of successful founders of high-growth US firms is 45, not 25. The startup myth of 22-year-old founders (Zuckerberg, Gates, Jobs) is media-selected; the data shows older founders are more common and have *higher* success rates per attempt, because experience matters.

**2. Domain experience predicts success.** Founders who built their company in an industry they had worked in *prior* succeed more often than founders entering a category cold. The mechanism: domain knowledge reduces the search space and provides early customers, suppliers, and credibility.

**3. Specific personality traits weakly predict outcomes.** Conscientiousness and openness-to-experience show small positive correlations with entrepreneurial outcomes; neuroticism shows small negative correlations. Extraversion is roughly null — extraverts and introverts succeed at similar rates. The "big five" is mostly noise at the individual-founder level; broader contextual variables (market timing, capital access, prior experience) dominate.

A useful synthesis: *traits* matter less than *context*. The conditions a founder enters — market timing, capital availability, team composition, prior experience — explain more variance than personality. The implication is consequential: improving the *system* (Week 10's innovation-ecosystem argument) does more for aggregate entrepreneurship than selecting individual founders ever can.

## Failure rates and survivorship bias

Two background facts every essay should acknowledge.

**Failure rates are high.** Of US venture-backed startups, roughly 60-75% return less than 1× capital; only ~10% return more than 5× (Cambridge Associates VC return data, longitudinal). Of *all* startups (most don't get VC), survival rates after 5 years are around 50% (US BLS data), but most "surviving" businesses are very small.

**Survivorship bias is pervasive.** Books and articles about startup success cover the successes — and the successful founders' habits, decisions, and personalities are inferred to be *causes* of success. The thousands of founders who did the same things and failed are invisible. The empirical methodology problem (no control group of failed-but-otherwise-comparable startups) means most "success factors" lists are correlations of *outcomes*, not predictors. Tim Hoff and others have written specifically about this; the broader literature on survivorship bias is foundational (Wald's WWII aircraft armouring story is the canonical example).

For exam essays: when citing a founder anecdote, acknowledge survivorship bias if relevant. A claim like "Airbnb's success shows X" should be paired with awareness that many startups did X and failed. The frameworks of Weeks 1-10 are more reliable than any single case because they were tested on many cases, including failures.

## The pitch deck: structure and slide-by-slide

The single most common founder artefact is the pitch deck — typically 10-15 slides used in investor meetings. The canonical structure (variants exist, but the components are stable):

```
Slide 1  ─ Title slide and tagline (one line about what you do)
Slide 2  ─ Problem
Slide 3  ─ Solution
Slide 4  ─ Market size (TAM/SAM/SOM)
Slide 5  ─ Product (demo or screenshots)
Slide 6  ─ Business model (how you make money)
Slide 7  ─ Traction (revenue, users, growth)
Slide 8  ─ Competition / why us
Slide 9  ─ Team
Slide 10 ─ The ask (how much, what for, milestones it funds)
```

Going through these slide-by-slide, what each *tests*:

**1. Tagline.** Tests whether the founder can compress the company into one sentence. Bad taglines are vague ("a platform for X"); good ones are concrete ("Airbnb: book accommodation in someone's home", "Slack: the messaging tool for teams").

**2. Problem.** Tests whether there is a *real, painful, frequent* problem. The Y Combinator instruction: write the problem as a *specific user with a specific symptom*, not as an abstract market trend.

**3. Solution.** Tests *fit* between problem and solution. The best solution slides are *one* sentence plus a screenshot. Verbal complexity here is a red flag — if the solution can't be explained simply, it usually can't be sold simply either.

**4. Market size.** TAM (total addressable market) is the full market in dollars per year; SAM (serviceable available market) is the subset the company can realistically reach; SOM (serviceable obtainable market) is what the company can capture in some near-term horizon. Investors look for *large* TAM (>$1B for VC-scale) but *credible* SOM (a specific path to capture meaningful share of a sub-market).

**5. Product.** Tests *executability*. A clean demo or screenshot grid signals the team can ship; a slide of feature bullet points signals they cannot.

**6. Business model.** Tests *unit economics* — revenue per customer, cost to acquire, contribution margin, payback period. Even early-stage investors want to see that the model can in principle make money.

**7. Traction.** This is the most-weighted slide. Real users, real revenue, real growth rate, real retention. "We just launched" is acceptable for very early stages; anything past pre-seed needs traction or a strong reason there isn't any.

**8. Competition.** Tests *self-awareness* and *strategic positioning*. The two failure modes: claiming no competition (always wrong — there is always at least the status quo) and presenting a 2×2 grid where you happen to be in the favourable quadrant (transparently constructed).

**9. Team.** Tests *who is doing this and why them*. Specific accomplishments, not titles. Cofounder relationships, prior startups, deep domain experience.

**10. The ask.** Tests *clarity of milestones*. "$3M to reach $1M ARR and 100 customers in 18 months" is testable; "raising a seed round" is not.

A common framing: a pitch deck is *not* a business plan. The deck is a *narrative artefact* designed to get a follow-up meeting; the data room (financial models, customer references, technical architecture) handles depth. Founders who try to fit the data room into the deck usually fail; founders who fit the *story* into the deck succeed.

## The Lean Canvas and Business Model Canvas (refresher)

Both canvases were introduced in Week 8 in the innovation-management context. Week 11's practitioner emphasis: which to use, when, and how.

**Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder 2010).** 9 blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key activities, key resources, key partnerships, cost structure. Suited to *established* business models — the blocks assume you know enough about the business to fill them in coherently. Useful for strategic planning, business-model innovation analysis, and explaining an existing business.

**Lean Canvas (Ash Maurya 2010).** 9 blocks but different ones: problem, customer segments, unique value proposition, solution, channels, revenue streams, cost structure, key metrics, unfair advantage. Suited to *early-stage* startups — the problem and metrics blocks force the founder to be specific about what they're testing.

In practice the difference is:

```
   Block (Business Model Canvas)     Block (Lean Canvas)
   ─────────────────────────────     ────────────────────
   Key Partners                      Problem
   Key Activities                    Solution
   Key Resources                     Key Metrics
   Value Propositions                Unique Value Proposition
   Customer Relationships            Unfair Advantage
   Channels                          Channels
   Customer Segments                 Customer Segments
   Cost Structure                    Cost Structure
   Revenue Streams                   Revenue Streams
```

The lean canvas swaps the *partners/activities/resources/relationships* blocks (which assume an operational business) for *problem/solution/metrics/unfair-advantage* (which assume a startup searching for product-market fit). For INFO4444 you should be comfortable with both; in startup contexts default to lean.

## Three skills the industry under-teaches

A common observation in industry-perspective lectures: technical founders graduate with strong product-development capability and weak *commercial* capability. Three specific skills the industry repeatedly flags.

**1. Sales.** Not "selling out" or "marketing fluff" — actual sales work: identifying buyers, qualifying interest, navigating a procurement process, closing transactions. Most CS curricula contain zero hours of sales training. Practitioner reading: Mark Roberge's *The Sales Acceleration Formula* (2015); Aaron Ross's *Predictable Revenue* (2011); the founder-led-sales framework articulated by Pete Kazanjy.

**2. Storytelling and presentation.** The ability to give a coherent 5-minute, 15-minute, and 60-minute version of the same story to different audiences. To run a board meeting. To pitch a customer. To explain the company in an internal all-hands. Practitioner reading: Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* and *Slide:ology*; Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick* (2007).

**3. Founder psychology and self-management.** Managing the emotional load of startup life. Practitioner reading: Ben Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* (2014); David Heinemeier Hansson and Jason Fried's *It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work* (2018) for the counter-cultural take; the Founders Network and similar peer-support groups.

A useful reframing: these are not "soft skills" — they are *commercial skills*. The technical-vs-commercial dichotomy that many CS students bring into industry experience is itself a learned bias that should be unlearned. The most effective tech founders treat sales, story, and psychology as *engineering problems* with their own frameworks, metrics, and iteration loops.

## CEO transitions

A specific topic that recurs in industry lectures: the transition from founder-CEO to professional-CEO. The pattern is well-documented:

- *Stage 0-1 (0-2 employees)*: founder does everything.
- *Stage 1-2 (3-20 employees)*: founder hires first executives (often a head of engineering, a first salesperson).
- *Stage 2-3 (20-100 employees)*: founder builds a functional leadership team.
- *Stage 3-4 (100-500 employees)*: tensions emerge — the founder's strengths (vision, customer instinct, technical taste) are not the same as the management capabilities the company now needs (operational planning, large-team management, financial discipline).
- *Stage 4 (500+ employees)*: many founders either grow into the CEO role (Jeff Bezos at Amazon, Elon Musk at Tesla, Mark Zuckerberg at Meta) or are replaced by external CEOs (Mike Cannon-Brookes/Scott Farquhar split CEO/COO duties at Atlassian; many YC-backed companies have founder-CEO transitions in years 5-7).

The Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas / Noam Wasserman research on founder displacement is the academic literature. Wasserman's *The Founder's Dilemmas* (2012) is the practitioner book. The empirical finding: founders who *plan* for the transition have better outcomes than founders who *resist* it.

## Australian founder narratives

Three Australian cases worth knowing by name and one-paragraph narrative.

**Atlassian (Mike Cannon-Brookes and Scott Farquhar, 2002-present).** Founded by two University of New South Wales graduates with a $10K credit card debt. Sold project-management software (Jira) to developers via the web, eschewing traditional enterprise sales. Bootstrapped to ~$10M revenue before raising any external capital. Raised a single $60M secondary round in 2010 (founders sold some shares; company received no primary capital). IPO'd on NASDAQ in 2015. Now ~$45B market cap. The model: high-velocity product-led growth, no enterprise sales force for many years, founder-owned majority through IPO. Anti-VC by structure; deeply Australian by origin; born global from week one (URL was atlassian.com from launch).

**Canva (Melanie Perkins and Cliff Obrecht, 2012-present).** Started as Fusion Books (an online yearbook designer for schools, 2008) — a vertical *MVP* that taught the founders the underlying technology. Pivoted to general-purpose design (Canva, 2012). Raised ~$580M across multiple rounds. Recent valuation ~$32B (private as of 2024). Australian-headquartered, born-global, growth led by product (free-tier viral adoption). Notable for staying private and Australian-based longer than most peers — bucking the capital-flight trend.

**WiseTech (Richard White, 1994-present).** Logistics software (CargoWise). Bootstrapped for ~20 years before listing on ASX in 2016. Now ~$25B market cap. The model: deep vertical software for global logistics carriers; sticky customer base; long sales cycles offset by high lifetime value. Demonstrates that durable Australian tech companies can be built without VC capital and without IPO'ing offshore.

**Counter-cases.** Many Australian tech successes had their main exit value flow offshore (Afterpay merged with Block on NYSE; Atlassian IPO'd on NASDAQ; LookSmart and many others were US-listed). The pattern reinforces Week 10's capital-flight observation.

## Practical takeaways for the assignment

If you are working on an entrepreneurial pitch (whether for an INFO4444 assignment or a real one), the operational checklist that emerges from Week 11 material:

1. **State the problem first**, before the solution. If you can't state the problem in one specific-customer-with-symptom sentence, return to customer development.
2. **Build the smallest MVP that tests the riskiest hypothesis.** Not the cheapest product you can build — the cheapest *experiment* you can run.
3. **Talk to 25 customers before writing the deck.** The deck is the *output* of customer discovery, not the input.
4. **Show traction, not features.** Investors weight traction more than vision. If traction is thin, *show* the learning velocity.
5. **Know the cap table before the round.** Model dilution forward two rounds before signing the term sheet.
6. **Pick the right canvas.** Lean canvas for early-stage; BMC for explaining a more mature business.
7. **Plan for failure.** Most experiments will fail. The success comes from the cumulative learning, not from any single experiment.
8. **Manage yourself.** Founder burnout kills more startups than any market factor. Sleep, exercise, peer support, professional help if needed.

## Founder archetypes and what each contributes

A useful synthesis from industry experience: successful early-stage teams typically combine three archetypes (which can be three people or two with overlapping skills).

**The Builder.** The person who can actually ship the product. Engineering depth, comfort with rapid iteration, willing to write throwaway code that learns rather than scales. Without a builder, the team builds nothing. Examples: Steve Wozniak at Apple (1976-85), Mike Cannon-Brookes at Atlassian (early years), Adam D'Angelo at Quora and earlier as Facebook CTO.

**The Seller.** The person who can find customers, convince them to pay, navigate procurement, close deals. Often externally focused, comfortable with rejection, instinctive about narrative and positioning. Examples: Steve Jobs (as marketer of Apple's products) at the company's founding, Scott Farquhar's commercial role at Atlassian, Stewart Butterfield's narrative leadership at Slack.

**The Operator.** The person who can scale process: hiring, finance, legal, operations, organisational design. Often added later but increasingly important from ~20 employees onward. Examples: Sheryl Sandberg at Facebook (joined 2008, age 39), Eric Schmidt at Google (joined 2001 as "adult supervision" for Brin/Page).

Most successful early-stage teams pair the Builder and Seller; the Operator joins as the company scales. Teams composed entirely of one archetype (three engineers, no salesperson; three salespeople, no engineer) reliably struggle.

A more refined model is Jim Collins's *Level 5 Leader* (*Good to Great*, 2001) — the founder who can grow into all three roles, exhibiting personal humility plus fierce resolve. Rare empirically; when it happens, the company often retains its founder-CEO long-term (Bezos at Amazon, Brin/Page at Google for many years, Reed Hastings at Netflix, Brian Chesky at Airbnb, Daniel Ek at Spotify).
