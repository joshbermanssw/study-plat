## What this week is really about

Week 13 is the *synthesis* week — a condensed cheatsheet across all twelve preceding weeks, side-by-side comparisons of the main frameworks, the most common exam-question patterns and how to answer them, and a quick course in essay-writing technique (PEEL paragraphs, signposting, the well-worn structure that gets marks on time-constrained exams). The point is not to teach new material — there is none — but to *consolidate* the unit's frameworks into structures you can actually deploy under exam pressure. By the end of the overview you should be able to (a) recite the unit's twelve "must-name" thinkers and what each contributed, (b) compare any two frameworks (Christensen vs Utterback-Abernathy, open vs closed innovation, lean vs stage-gate) along their key dimensions, (c) recognise the three or four standard exam-question patterns, and (d) apply the PEEL paragraph structure to a worked example.

## Comparison table 1: Christensen vs Utterback-Abernathy

The two foundational descriptive frameworks of the unit. Many exam questions ask you to apply one or both to a case. Knowing how they relate is essential.

| Dimension | Utterback-Abernathy (1975) | Christensen (1997) |
|-----------|----------------------------|--------------------|
| Focal question | How does an industry's technology evolve? | Why do incumbents fail at new technology? |
| Unit of analysis | Industry / product category | Firm + market segment |
| Time horizon | Long (decades) | Medium (years) |
| Phases | Fluid → Transitional → Specific | Sustaining → Disruptive entry → Market tipping |
| Mechanism | Selection of dominant design via learning + network effects | Resource allocation favours mainstream customers, blocks disruptive bets |
| Key empirical base | US automobiles, 1894-1923; later replicated in many industries | Disk drives, 1976-1994; later steel, retail, airlines |
| Predicts incumbent success when... | Innovation is incremental within dominant design | Innovation is sustaining (same dimensions, larger improvements) |
| Predicts incumbent failure when... | Discontinuity occurs and old competences are destroyed | Innovation is disruptive (worse on existing dimensions, better on new) |
| Strongest critique | Not all industries produce single dominant designs; phase boundaries fuzzy | Lepore 2014: cherry-picked cases; King-Baatartogtokh 2015: only 9% of cases fit |

The frameworks *complement* rather than compete. UA explains the *trajectory* inside an era of incremental change; Christensen explains the *transition* between trajectories. The Anderson-Tushman extension of UA already had a disruption-like concept (competence-destroying discontinuities); Christensen sharpened the *mechanism* (resource-allocation theory inside the firm).

## Comparison table 2: Open vs closed innovation

Useful for any question on innovation strategy or IP/openness trade-offs.

| Dimension | Closed innovation | Open innovation |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Locus of R&D | Internal labs | Internal + external networks |
| IP strategy | Defensive secrecy or block | IP as currency; licensing in and out |
| Talent | Hire and retain best people | Tap external talent through partnerships |
| Distribution | Vertically integrated | Multiple channels including partners |
| Time to market | Slower (do it all yourself) | Faster (combine internal + external) |
| Capital efficiency | Lower (you pay for everything) | Higher (shared R&D cost) |
| Risk profile | Concentrated | Distributed |
| Best when... | High-tech with strong IP protection (pharma), narrow technical scope | Modular technology, fast-clockspeed, multiple complementary capabilities |
| Apple-style closure works when... | Premium positioning, deep system integration, brand power | (Apple is an open *inputs* + closed *system* hybrid) |
| Required complement | Strong internal capability | Absorptive capacity (Cohen-Levinthal) |
| Failure mode | Hollowing out (Xerox PARC effect) | Loss of distinct competence; rebranded outsourcing |

The synthesis you should be able to articulate: open is not *better* than closed in some absolute sense. The right choice depends on technology characteristics (modular vs integral), industry clockspeed (fast vs slow), and firm capability (deep R&D vs narrow). Modern firms typically use *both* strategically — open at the component level, closed at the system level.

## Comparison table 3: Lean Startup vs Stage-Gate

The Week 7 vs Week 8 contrast. Many exam questions ask which is appropriate for a given context.

| Dimension | Lean Startup (Ries 2011) | Stage-Gate (Cooper 1986) |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Target organisation | Startup; new business unit | Mature firm; established product lines |
| Hypothesis status | Unknown — to be discovered | Known — to be executed |
| Cycle time | Days to weeks per loop | Months per gate |
| Investment per cycle | Small | Larger and growing through stages |
| Customer involvement | Continuous (build-measure-learn) | Periodic (at gates) |
| Failure tolerance | Pivots expected and normal | Kill at gate is the failure mode |
| Optimisation goal | Validated learning per dollar | Net present value per project |
| Best for | Disruptive, new-category, pre-product-market-fit | Incremental, adjacent, post-product-market-fit |
| Failure mode | Over-iteration; loss of strategic vision | Killing disruptive opportunities at finance gates |
| Hybrid pattern | Apply lean inside early gates; stage-gate the scale-up phase | Stage-gate the core; lean experimentation in dedicated explore unit |

The honours-level synthesis: large organisations need *both* methodologies, applied to *different parts of the portfolio*. The ambidextrous organisation (O'Reilly-Tushman) is the structural answer; the governance challenge is matching method to opportunity and protecting non-standard methods politically.

## The four standard exam question patterns

INFO4444 exam questions (and similar units' exams) tend to come in four flavours. Recognising the pattern lets you pre-plan a structure before writing.

**Pattern 1: Classify a case study on the curve.** Example: "Apply Utterback-Abernathy's framework to the smart-speaker market in 2024. What phase is it in?"

Structure:
- *Introduction*: state the framework briefly with citation.
- *Body 1*: dimension 1 (product) — apply with evidence.
- *Body 2*: dimension 2 (process).
- *Body 3*: dimension 3 (organisation).
- *Body 4*: dimension 4 (competitive environment).
- *Conclusion*: state the phase with justification and one limitation of the framework's application here.

**Pattern 2: Strategy recommendation.** Example: "Design an IP strategy for a small Australian SaaS startup."

Structure:
- *Introduction*: name the firm context and the strategic question.
- *Body 1*: copyright (automatic, baseline protection for code).
- *Body 2*: trademarks (brand protection, low cost, durable).
- *Body 3*: trade secrets (server-side algorithms, customer data, internal methods).
- *Body 4*: patents (case-by-case; high cost; consider only for distinctive technical inventions).
- *Body 5*: open-source strategy (which licence; what to release).
- *Conclusion*: a coherent integrated recommendation with priorities.

**Pattern 3: Funding-path question.** Example: "Recommend a funding strategy for a deep-tech hardware startup at the prototype stage."

Structure:
- *Introduction*: characterise the stage (prototype, pre-revenue) and the capital needs.
- *Body 1*: Australian-specific non-dilutive (RDTI, grants, CRC-P).
- *Body 2*: pre-seed / angel / accelerator (Y Combinator, Startmate).
- *Body 3*: seed round, valuation expectation, instrument choice (SAFE vs note vs priced).
- *Body 4*: Series A and beyond — what milestones unlock each.
- *Body 5*: exit planning (M&A vs IPO; what success looks like).
- *Conclusion*: prioritised, sequenced recommendation.

**Pattern 4: Critical-evaluation essay.** Example: "Critically evaluate Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation."

Structure:
- *Introduction*: state the theory's claim and significance.
- *Body 1*: explain the framework in technical detail.
- *Body 2*: present supporting evidence — strong cases.
- *Body 3*: present critiques — Lepore, King-Baatartogtokh.
- *Body 4*: balanced synthesis — what survives, what doesn't.
- *Conclusion*: a position you can defend, acknowledging the limits.

For each pattern, *prepare a structural template in advance*. Going into the exam with templates lets you spend cognitive effort on content rather than structure.

## PEEL paragraph structure

The most reliable paragraph-writing structure for time-constrained exam essays.

**P — Point**. State the claim of the paragraph in one clear sentence at the start.

**E — Evidence**. Provide specific evidence: a name (thinker), a year (publication), a case (firm), a number (data point).

**E — Explanation / Elaboration**. Explain *why* the evidence supports the claim. This is where most undergraduate paragraphs fall short — they assert the point and present the evidence but skip the connective tissue that says "*therefore*..."

**L — Link**. Link back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph. This signals to the marker that you understand the argument's overall direction.

Worked PEEL example. Topic: dominant design in personal computers.

> *(Point)* The IBM PC's emergence as the dominant design in personal computing illustrates Utterback and Abernathy's claim that the winning configuration is rarely the technologically superior one. *(Evidence)* When IBM launched the PC in 1981, the Apple II offered better graphics, the Xerox Alto a superior user interface, and minicomputers more capability — yet within five years the IBM PC architecture (and its clones) had captured the dominant share of the personal-computer market, a position it held for nearly two decades. *(Explanation)* The PC won not because of technical merit but because IBM's open architecture, combined with Intel's processor and Microsoft's operating system, allowed clone makers to compete on price and assemblers to compete on configuration, producing a dense ecosystem of complementary products and learning effects that compounded into a self-reinforcing cycle of cost and adoption. *(Link)* This pattern — dominant designs winning on ecosystem rather than performance — recurs in later technology categories including smartphones, where the iOS-Android codominance was determined by ecosystem strategy rather than raw device superiority, and predicts that early-stage assessments of new categories (AI agents, AR/VR) should focus more on ecosystem dynamics than on technical benchmarks.

The paragraph hits a *named claim* (Utterback-Abernathy), *specific evidence* (the PC's launch year, named competitors), *causal explanation* (why this happened), and a *link* that previews and previews the next paragraph or the broader thesis.

## Signposting

Markers reading 40 essays in a row will reward *signposting* — sentences that tell the reader where they are in the argument. Useful signpost templates:

- *"This essay argues that..."* (thesis-setting)
- *"Three considerations support this..."* (preview structure)
- *"The first consideration is..."* (introduce body section)
- *"While the framework is widely cited, three critiques deserve engagement..."* (transition to critique)
- *"In sum..."* (begin conclusion)

Signposting is *not* padding. It is genuine information about the argument's structure that helps the reader (and marker) follow you. In a time-constrained exam, signposting saves marks by reducing the marker's cognitive load.

## A worked exam answer outline

Imagine the question: *"Using the frameworks covered in this unit, evaluate whether open-source software development is a sustainable model for delivering critical infrastructure. (25 marks, 40 minutes)"*

Outline you should sketch in 3 minutes before writing:

1. *Introduction (3-4 sentences)*: state that open-source has delivered critical infrastructure (Linux runs the cloud, OpenSSL secures the web) — sustainability is contested. Frame the answer using Chesbrough's open innovation, von Hippel's user innovation, the licence and governance frameworks of Week 5, and a critique grounded in the post-Heartbleed maintenance literature.

2. *Body 1: open-source's strengths (Chesbrough + von Hippel + Raymond)*. Distributed innovation, user-as-innovator, "many eyes" argument, network effects in adoption.

3. *Body 2: governance and economic sustainability (foundation vs corporate-led)*. Linux Foundation (sustained by corporate dues), Mozilla (sustained by Google search royalties), the long-tail maintenance problem.

4. *Body 3: failure cases (Heartbleed, Log4Shell, the maintainer-burnout literature)*. Critical infrastructure projects maintained by tiny under-funded teams. Tarleton Gillespie and Mary Gray's labour critique.

5. *Body 4: the licence ecosystem (GPL vs Apache vs source-available licences)*. Recent moves (MongoDB, Elastic, HashiCorp) to capture cloud-vendor revenue and the resulting forks. Open source is sustainable but the *boundaries* of "open" are renegotiating.

6. *Conclusion*: nuanced — open-source delivers critical infrastructure today, but the *funding model* for maintenance is fragile and the recent licence wars suggest the field is mid-transition. Recommendations: strengthen foundations, fund critical-infrastructure maintainers explicitly (the Sovereign Tech Fund model in Germany), accept that purely volunteer maintenance is not sufficient at the scale of modern dependencies.

That outline takes 3 minutes and contains every named framework needed. Writing then takes 25-30 minutes. Reviewing takes 5 minutes. Total 35 minutes — within the 40-minute budget. The structure is the answer.

## Time management in the exam

A rough rule for INFO4444-shaped exams (often 2-hour, multiple essays):

- *3-5 minutes* per essay outlining structure before writing.
- *25-30 minutes* writing.
- *3-5 minutes* reading back to catch missing citations and unclear sentences.

Resist the temptation to write the entire answer in 40 continuous minutes. The outline phase pays for itself many times over: an outlined essay scores higher than the same content unstructured.
