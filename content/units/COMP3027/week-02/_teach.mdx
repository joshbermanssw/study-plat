## What greedy means, in one sentence

**A greedy algorithm builds a solution one piece at a time, picking whatever looks best *right now* by some fixed rule, and never revisiting that choice.**

Greedy is appealing because:

- It's fast (usually one pass over the input).
- It's easy to code.
- The pseudocode often fits in five lines.

It's also treacherous, because *most* problems don't admit a greedy optimum. The hard part isn't writing the algorithm — it's proving the algorithm is correct.

This week you'll learn the proof technique you'll use most in this course: the **exchange argument**. You'll also meet six classic greedy problems whose solutions are worth memorising. For each one, be ready to:

1. State the problem precisely (inputs, outputs, objective).
2. Write the greedy in pseudocode.
3. Sketch the optimality proof.
4. Cite the runtime with the data structure that achieves it.

## When does greedy work? Two ingredients

Greedy works exactly when two properties hold.

**1. Greedy choice property.** Some locally optimal choice is contained in *some* globally optimal solution. (Note: it doesn't have to be in *every* optimum — just *one*.)

**2. Optimal substructure.** After you make the greedy choice, the leftover subproblem's optimum combines with your choice to give the full optimum.

When both hold, you can prove correctness by induction: take the greedy choice, recurse on the residual, combine.

**Watch out:** optimal substructure alone is not enough. Both shortest paths *and* longest paths in a DAG have optimal substructure, but only shortest paths admits a greedy. The greedy choice property is the harder condition, and it's where exchange arguments earn their keep.

## The exchange argument, with a full worked proof

This is the proof technique. Here it is on interval scheduling.

### Problem: interval scheduling

**Input.** n requests, each an interval [sᵢ, fᵢ).
**Output.** A maximum-size set S of requests that pairwise don't overlap.

### The greedy: earliest finish time first

```text
schedule(intervals):
  sort by finish time
  S = []
  lastFinish = -infinity
  for [s, f] in sorted order:
    if s >= lastFinish:
      S.append([s, f])
      lastFinish = f
  return S
```

**The claim.** S is a maximum-size compatible subset.

### The proof, step by step

We're going to compare the greedy's picks against an optimal solution's picks side by side, and show **the greedy is never behind**. Once we have that, the rest follows.

**Setup.** Two lists of intervals to compare:

- **G** = g₁, g₂, ..., gₖ — what the greedy picked, in the order it picked them. The greedy ended up with `k` picks total.
- **O** = o₁, o₂, ..., oₘ — what some optimal solution picked, sorted by finish time. The optimal ended up with `m` picks total.

Our goal: show that `k = m`. (Then the greedy is just as good as the optimal.)

The variable **r** is just a counter — round 1, round 2, ..., up to round k. At round r we compare the greedy's r-th pick gᵣ against the optimal's r-th pick oᵣ.

> **Stays-ahead claim:** at every round r from 1 to k, `finish(gᵣ) ≤ finish(oᵣ)`.
>
> In a picture:
>
> ```text
> optimal:  o₁───   o₂───       o₃───       o₄───
> greedy:   g₁──    g₂──        g₃──        g₄──
> ```
>
> The greedy's r-th pick always finishes no later than the optimal's r-th pick.

We prove the claim by induction on r.

**Base case — round 1.** The greedy's very first move is "pick the request that finishes earliest in the whole input." Nothing finishes earlier than that, not even o₁. So `finish(g₁) ≤ finish(o₁)`. ✓

**Inductive step — round r + 1.** Assume we've proved it up to round r: `finish(gᵣ) ≤ finish(oᵣ)`. Now we show it still holds at round r + 1.

Walking through the chain in plain English:

1. `o_(r+1)` is the optimal's next pick. Because O is sorted by finish time and the picks don't overlap, `o_(r+1)` must start at or after `finish(oᵣ)`.
2. By the induction hypothesis, `finish(gᵣ) ≤ finish(oᵣ)`. Chain those: `o_(r+1)` also starts at or after `finish(gᵣ)`.
3. That means `o_(r+1)` doesn't overlap any of the greedy's first r picks (they all finished by `finish(gᵣ)`). So `o_(r+1)` is one of the requests the greedy *could* pick at round r + 1.
4. The greedy's rule is "of the compatible options still on the table, pick the one with the smallest finish time." It might pick `o_(r+1)` exactly, or it might find something with an even smaller finish time. Either way: `finish(g_(r+1)) ≤ finish(o_(r+1))`. ✓

So by induction, the stays-ahead claim holds at every round.

**Now the punchline — why k = m.** Suppose for contradiction the optimal got more picks, `m > k`. Then there's an `o_(k+1)` waiting. By stays-ahead, `finish(gₖ) ≤ finish(oₖ) ≤ start(o_(k+1))`. So `o_(k+1)` is compatible with everything the greedy already picked. But then the greedy *would not have stopped* at gₖ — it would have grabbed o_(k+1) (or something even better). Contradiction. So `m ≤ k`. And of course `k ≤ m` (the optimal is by definition the most you can pick). Therefore **k = m**. ∎

**Why is this called an "exchange argument"?** You can read the same proof as a swap procedure. Take any optimal O. Swap o₁ for g₁ — we showed g₁ finishes no later, so the swap can't break anything that came after. Swap o₂ for g₂. After k swaps you've turned O into G with the same size. So G must be optimal too.

**Runtime.** Sorting dominates: Θ(n log n). The scan is Θ(n). Total: **Θ(n log n)**.

## Interval partitioning: how many rooms do you need?

Same setup as before — n intervals on a timeline — but now you have to schedule **all** of them, not pick a subset. Lectures need rooms. The question is: **what's the fewest number of rooms you need so that no two lectures in the same room overlap?**

A picture of 5 lecture intervals:

```text
time:    9   10  11  12  1   2   3   4
A:       ─────────
B:           ──────
C:                       ───────
D:                  ───────
E:               ─────────────
```

At 11am, lectures A, B, and E are all running at the same time. So at that moment you need at least **3 rooms**. (You can't squeeze 3 simultaneous lectures into 2 rooms.) That number — the maximum simultaneous-overlap count — is the lower bound for your answer.

We give that number a name:

> **Depth d** = the maximum number of intervals that overlap at any single point in time.

In the picture, d = 3 (at 11am).

**Claim.** The minimum number of rooms is exactly d.

> *(Side note — why some textbooks say "colours" instead of "rooms": this problem is also called "interval graph colouring", where each interval is a vertex, edges connect overlapping intervals, and a "colour" is just a label you assign to a vertex so that no two connected vertices share a colour. "Room" = "colour" = "label" = "class". We'll stick with "rooms".)*

**Lower bound (you need at least d rooms).** At the moment when d intervals overlap, every one of them needs a different room — they're all running simultaneously. So you can't get away with fewer than d rooms.

**Upper bound (d rooms suffice).** Here's a greedy algorithm that uses exactly d:

```text
partition(intervals):
  sort intervals by start time
  for each interval I in order:
    if some room's last lecture ended at or before start(I):
      put I in that room (reuse it)
    else:
      open a brand new room for I
```

**Why this uses no more than d rooms.** Suppose the greedy opens a new room — call it room number k — for some interval I. Why couldn't it reuse rooms 1, 2, ..., k − 1?

Because at the moment `start(I)`, every one of those k − 1 rooms still has an interval running (none of them have finished by `start(I)`). Add I itself and you have **k intervals all overlapping at the point `start(I)`**. So the depth d is at least k — i.e. d ≥ k.

Combine with the lower bound d ≤ (number of rooms used), and you get: **rooms used = d**.

**Runtime.** Sort once (Θ(n log n)) + a min-heap keyed by "next free time" of each room to do the reuse check in O(log n) per interval. Total: **Θ(n log n)**.

## Scheduling to minimise lateness (full exchange proof)

**Problem.** n jobs, each with processing time tᵢ and deadline dᵢ. Schedule them non-preemptively on one machine starting at t = 0. If job i finishes at time fᵢ, its lateness is Lᵢ = max(0, fᵢ − dᵢ). Minimise Lₘₐₓ = maxᵢ Lᵢ.

**Greedy: Earliest Deadline First (EDF).** Sort by dᵢ ascending, run back-to-back.

### The exchange proof

Define an **inversion**: a pair (i, j) where i is scheduled before j but dᵢ &gt; dⱼ.

**Lemma 1.** If a schedule has any inversion, it has an *adjacent* inversion.

*Proof.* Follow deadlines from i along the schedule to j. The sequence starts at dᵢ, ends at dⱼ, with dᵢ &gt; dⱼ. Somewhere two consecutive deadlines must drop — that's an adjacent inversion. ∎

**Lemma 2.** Swapping an adjacent inverted pair doesn't increase Lₘₐₓ.

*Proof.* Suppose the schedule shows ... i, j ... with dᵢ &gt; dⱼ. Let fⱼ be j's finish time before the swap, and let "prefix time" be the time when this pair starts.

Before: Lᵢ = (prefix + tᵢ) − dᵢ, Lⱼ = (prefix + tᵢ + tⱼ) − dⱼ = fⱼ − dⱼ.
After (swap to j, i): Lⱼ' = (prefix + tⱼ) − dⱼ, Lᵢ' = (prefix + tⱼ + tᵢ) − dᵢ = fⱼ − dᵢ.

Other jobs aren't affected. The new max of these two is Lᵢ' = fⱼ − dᵢ.

Since dⱼ &lt; dᵢ: Lᵢ' = fⱼ − dᵢ &lt; fⱼ − dⱼ = Lⱼ.

So the swap's max is ≤ the old max. No increase. ∎

**Theorem.** EDF is optimal.

*Proof.* Let S* be any optimum. Repeatedly swap adjacent inversions (no harm per Lemma 2). You eventually reach the unique inversion-free schedule — EDF. So Lₘₐₓ(EDF) ≤ Lₘₐₓ(S*). ∎

**Why no idle time?** Removing idle time only makes things finish earlier, so it can't increase any lateness.

**Runtime.** Sorting ⇒ **Θ(n log n)**.

## Huffman coding

**Problem.** Given symbols a₁, ..., aₙ with frequencies pᵢ &gt; 0, build a prefix-free binary code minimising L = Σ pᵢ ℓᵢ, where ℓᵢ is the codeword length for aᵢ.

**Greedy idea.** Two least-frequent symbols should be deepest in the code tree (they cost the most per bit, so we want their codewords short — wait, actually long, since infrequent symbols can afford long codewords).

```text
huffman(P):
  H = min-heap of (frequency, leaf-node) for each symbol
  while H has more than one entry:
    (p1, x) = extract_min
    (p2, y) = extract_min
    z = new internal node with children x, y
    insert (p1 + p2, z) into H
  return the root
```

**Why it's correct (sketch — two lemmas).**

1. **Greedy choice.** In some optimum, the two least-frequent symbols are siblings at maximum depth. (If not, swap them down — codeword lengths swap, but the cost can only get better because we're trading deeper positions for less-frequent symbols.)

2. **Optimal substructure.** Merge the two least-frequent symbols into a virtual symbol with summed frequency. The optimum of the merged instance combines with the merge into the optimum of the original.

Induction on n closes it.

**Runtime.** **Θ(n log n)** using a binary heap.

## Minimum spanning tree (MST)

**Problem.** A connected weighted graph G = (V, E, w). Find a spanning tree T of minimum total weight.

### The two key lemmas

First, one word of vocabulary that the lemmas need.

> **A cut** of a graph is just a way of splitting the vertices into two non-empty groups: call them S and V \ S (everything not in S). An edge "crosses the cut" if one endpoint is in S and the other isn't.
>
> ```text
>   S = {a, b, c}    V \ S = {d, e}
>   a────b   ┊   d
>   │    │   ┊   │
>   c────┼───┼───e        ← these dashed-line edges cross the cut
>        b───┊───d
> ```
>
> A cut doesn't have to be "meaningful"; it's just any 2-colouring of the vertices. Different cuts give different sets of crossing edges.

Now the two lemmas:

**Cut property.** Pick any cut (S, V \ S). Among the edges crossing that cut, the **lightest one** belongs to *some* MST.

**Cycle property.** Pick any cycle. The **heaviest edge** in that cycle is in *no* MST.

Both come from exchange arguments. (Cut: take any MST not containing the min-cut-edge e; adding e creates a cycle; that cycle must contain another edge that crosses the cut, and it's at least as heavy as e — remove it. You've produced a spanning tree whose total weight is no greater. So an MST containing e exists.)

### Kruskal's algorithm

Sort edges ascending; for each, include it unless it creates a cycle (use union-find to check).

```text
kruskal(G):
  sort E by weight
  for each v: makeset(v)
  T = []
  for (u, v, w) in sorted E:
    if find(u) != find(v):
      T.append((u, v)); union(u, v)
  return T
```

**Correctness.** Each accepted edge is the lightest edge crossing the cut between its endpoints' components and the rest. Cut property says it's in some MST.

**Runtime.** **Θ(m log n)** — dominated by sorting; union-find is essentially O(α(n)) ≈ O(1) per op.

### Prim's algorithm

Grow T from a start vertex; always add the lightest edge with exactly one endpoint in T.

```text
prim(G, s):
  key[s] = 0; key[v] = +inf otherwise
  Q = min-heap over V keyed by key[v]
  while Q not empty:
    u = extract_min(Q)
    add (parent[u], u) to T (except root)
    for each (u, v):
      if v in Q and w(u,v) < key[v]:
        key[v] = w(u,v); parent[v] = u; decrease_key
```

**Correctness.** Each iteration adds the min edge across the cut (T, V \ T). Cut property.

**Runtime.** Binary heap: **O((n + m) log n)**. Fibonacci heap: **O(m + n log n)**.

### Bonus: reverse-delete

The dual of Kruskal. Sort edges *descending*; remove each edge if removing it keeps the graph connected.

**Correctness via cycle property.** Each removed edge is the max-weight edge in some cycle.

**Runtime.** O(m log m) sort + O(m·(m+n)) for connectivity checks. Slower than Kruskal, but conceptually clean.

## Dijkstra's shortest paths

**Problem.** Directed graph, non-negative edge weights, source s. Compute δ(s, v) for every v.

```text
dijkstra(G, s):
  d[v] = +inf for v != s; d[s] = 0
  Q = min-heap of (d[v], v)
  while Q not empty:
    u = extract_min
    for each edge (u, v):
      if d[u] + w(u,v) < d[v]:
        d[v] = d[u] + w(u,v); decrease_key(Q, v)
```

### Correctness proof (the "relaxation" idea)

Quick vocabulary check before the proof.

> **Edge relaxation** is the one-line update Dijkstra does every time it processes an edge `(x, y)`:
>
> ```text
> if d[x] + w(x, y) < d[y]:
>     d[y] = d[x] + w(x, y)
>     predecessor[y] = x
> ```
>
> In English: *"if going via x is shorter than the best route to y we've found so far, update the best route to go via x"*. Each individual relaxation might be wrong, but the array `d` only ever gets smaller, so it can only get *closer* to the truth.
>
> Dijkstra's correctness is the claim that **by the time a vertex is finalised (extracted from the heap), its `d` value is the true shortest distance**.

**Claim.** When u is extracted, d[u] = δ(s, u).

**Proof by contradiction.** Suppose u is the *first* vertex extracted with d[u] &gt; δ(s, u). Take any shortest s-u path π. Walk along π and let y be the first vertex not yet in S (the finalised set), with predecessor x ∈ S (x exists because s ∈ S).

1. When x was added to S, we relaxed the edge (x, y). So d[y] ≤ d[x] + w(x, y).
2. By assumption, u is the *first* to be extracted with the wrong distance, and x was extracted earlier, so d[x] = δ(s, x).
3. Subpaths of shortest paths are shortest, so δ(s, x) + w(x, y) = δ(s, y).
4. Therefore d[y] ≤ δ(s, y) ≤ δ(s, u) &lt; d[u].
5. But Dijkstra extracted u before y, so d[u] ≤ d[y]. Contradiction. ∎

**Why non-negative edge weights matter.** Step 3 uses that subpath weights only grow as you extend, which fails with negative edges. For negative weights, use Bellman-Ford (Week 5).

**Runtime.** Binary heap **O((n + m) log n)**, Fibonacci heap **O(m + n log n)**, no heap (linear scan) **O(n²)**.

## When greedy fails: a counterexample

The classic failure: **coin change with non-standard denominations.**

Take coins &#123;1, 3, 4&#125; and target value 6.

- **Greedy** (always take the largest coin ≤ remaining): 4 + 1 + 1 = **3 coins**.
- **Optimum**: 3 + 3 = **2 coins**.

Greedy choice property fails: the largest coin isn't in any optimum here. So we'll solve coin change with dynamic programming in Week 4/5.

**Subtler failure: 0/1 knapsack vs fractional knapsack.** Fractional knapsack admits a greedy by density (value/weight). 0/1 knapsack doesn't — taking the densest item might block a strictly better combination. If you understand *why this difference exists*, you understand the greedy choice property.

## The exchange-argument recipe

When you're asked to prove a greedy is optimal, follow this template.

1. **State the problem.** Input, output, objective, feasibility.
2. **State the greedy.** What rule selects the next element?
3. **State the invariant.** Usually: "after k iterations, the greedy's partial solution can be extended to an optimum" or "any optimum can be transformed into the greedy without harming the objective".
4. **Prove the invariant by induction.** Base case (k = 0 trivial); inductive step (show the greedy's choice doesn't force suboptimality — swap in the greedy element for whatever the optimum did).
5. **Conclude.** When the invariant collapses, greedy = optimum.
6. **State runtime** with the data structure.

If you can't complete step 4, your greedy criterion is probably wrong — or the problem isn't actually greedy.

## All the greedies at a glance

| Problem | Greedy rule | Proof | Runtime |
|---------|-------------|-------|---------|
| Interval scheduling | Earliest finish time | Exchange / invariant | Θ(n log n) |
| Interval partitioning | Earliest free room | Lower bound = depth | Θ(n log n) |
| Min lateness | Earliest deadline | Adjacent-inversion exchange | Θ(n log n) |
| Huffman | Merge two least-frequent | Two-lemma swap | Θ(n log n) |
| Kruskal MST | Lightest non-cycle edge | Cut property | Θ(m log n) |
| Prim MST | Lightest edge into tree | Cut property | Θ(m + n log n) |
| Dijkstra SSSP | Closest unfinished | Relaxation invariant | Θ(m + n log n) |

## Union-find, for Kruskal

Kruskal's runtime hinges on union-find. Three operations:

- `makeset(x)`: create a singleton &#123;x&#125;.
- `find(x)`: return x's representative.
- `union(x, y)`: merge the two sets.

**Implementation: union by rank + path compression.**

- Each element points to a parent. The *root* is the representative.
- Rank is an upper bound on tree height.
- `union` attaches the lower-rank tree under the higher-rank root.
- `find` walks to the root, then re-parents every node it visited *directly* to the root.

**Amortised cost (Tarjan 1975).** m operations on n elements: **O(m · α(n))**, where α is the inverse Ackermann function. For all practical n, α(n) ≤ 4, so this is effectively O(m).
